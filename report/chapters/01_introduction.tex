\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Background}

Human activity recognition (HAR) is the task of inferring what a person is
doing from sensor observations. It underpins assisted-living systems, fall
detection for the elderly, patient monitoring, and smart-building
automation. The dominant sensing modalities each carry a cost that limits
where they can be deployed.

\textbf{Cameras} give the richest signal but are unacceptable in bedrooms
and bathrooms --- precisely the rooms where falls are most likely. They also
fail in darkness and behind occlusions, and storing video of a private home
creates a data-protection liability that a small deployment cannot
discharge.

\textbf{Wearables} (accelerometers, smartwatches, pendant alarms) sidestep
the privacy problem but depend on compliance. The device must be worn,
charged, and not removed --- and the population most in need of fall
detection is the population least likely to satisfy all three, particularly
at night and while bathing.

\textbf{Ambient radio sensing} avoids both failure modes. A WiFi transmitter
already present in the room illuminates the space continuously; a receiver
observes how the human body perturbs the propagation of that signal. Nothing
is worn, nothing is recorded that resembles an image, and the sensing works
in darkness and through soft furnishings.

\subsection{From RSSI to Channel State Information}

The earliest WiFi sensing work used the \emph{Received Signal Strength
Indicator} (RSSI), a single scalar per packet summarising total received
power. RSSI is coarse: it collapses all propagation paths into one number,
so two very different multipath configurations that happen to deliver the
same total energy are indistinguishable. It is also unstable, because
transmitter rate adaptation and automatic gain control move it for reasons
unrelated to the environment.

\emph{Channel State Information} (CSI) is the finer-grained alternative
exposed by OFDM receivers. An 802.11n/ac receiver estimates the complex
channel response $H_k$ on each of its OFDM subcarriers in order to equalise
the received symbols; CSI is that per-subcarrier estimate made visible to
software. Instead of one scalar, each packet yields a vector of complex
values --- a sampled frequency response of the radio channel:
%
\begin{equation}
  y_k = H_k x_k + n_k, \qquad k = 1 \dots K,
\end{equation}
%
where $x_k$ and $y_k$ are the transmitted and received symbols on subcarrier
$k$ and $n_k$ is noise. Because the subcarriers are spread across the
channel bandwidth, and because multipath components with different delays
produce different phase rotations across frequency, $\{H_k\}$ encodes the
\emph{structure} of the propagation environment, not merely its total
attenuation.

A moving human body changes that structure. Limbs and torso reflect,
scatter, and block propagation paths; the resulting time-varying multipath
appears in the CSI amplitude series as motion-specific patterns, with
Doppler content determined by limb velocity. Walking, sitting down, and
falling perturb the channel in measurably different ways, which is what
makes classification possible.

The practical obstacle for a long time was access: CSI was only readable on
a handful of specialised NICs (notably the Intel 5300 with a patched driver,
and Atheros chipsets). The arrival of CSI APIs on low-cost microcontroller
WiFi SoCs, including Espressif's ESP32 family, removed that barrier and put
CSI sensing within reach of a student project budget.

\section{Problem Statement}

This project recognises five human activities --- \emph{walking},
\emph{sitting}, \emph{standing}, \emph{lying}, and \emph{falling} --- plus a
sixth \emph{background} class denoting an empty room, from the CSI amplitude
observed by three ESP32-S3 receivers illuminated by a single commodity WiFi
router.

Three constraints shape every design decision that follows.

\textbf{The hardware is single-antenna.} Each ESP32-S3 has one antenna and a
consumer-grade crystal oscillator. The raw CSI phase is therefore corrupted
by carrier frequency offset, sampling frequency offset, and packet detection
delay, and the standard sanitisation trick --- taking phase differences
across antennas on the same NIC, which cancels the common offset --- is
unavailable. The pipeline is consequently \textbf{amplitude-only}. This is
not a shortcut; it is the same choice made by essentially all credible
published ESP32 CSI work.

\textbf{Falling is safety-critical and asymmetric.} A missed fall is a
qualitatively worse error than a false alarm, and falls are simultaneously
the scarcest class to collect, since each sample requires a mattress and a
spotter. Falling recall is therefore reported separately throughout this
report rather than being absorbed into an overall accuracy figure, and the
training loss is class-weighted.

\textbf{Honest evaluation is harder than high accuracy.} The standard way to
report CSI HAR results --- shuffling fixed-length windows randomly into
train and test sets --- leaks information. Consecutive windows overlap in
time and are drawn from the same activity bout, the same clothing, and the
same instantaneous furniture geometry, so a random split places
near-duplicate windows on both sides of the partition. The resulting
accuracy, typically 95--99\%, measures memorisation as much as recognition,
and collapses when the system meets a new person or a new day. This project
therefore commits in advance to reporting three splits for every model, and
treats the gap between them as a result in its own right rather than a
defect to be hidden.

\section{Objectives}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Build a 3-receiver ESP32-S3 CSI acquisition system (100 packets/s).
  \item Collect a labeled multi-subject, multi-session activity dataset.
  \item Train and compare classical and deep models (SVM/RF baseline,
        CNN, CNN-LSTM) on amplitude spectrograms.
  \item Achieve $\geq 85\%$ accuracy on random and cross-session splits;
        quantify the cross-subject generalization gap honestly.
  \item Demonstrate real-time recognition with temporal smoothing and a
        live dashboard, including a fall alert.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope and Limitations}

The following bounds are set deliberately, and are stated here so that the
evaluation in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} is read against the right claim.

\textbf{In scope.} A single person in the sensing area; indoor rooms of
roughly domestic scale with the receivers and router in fixed, measured
positions; the six classes listed above; amplitude features only;
recognition on a host laptop fed by USB-serial from three receivers.

\textbf{Explicitly out of scope for this version.}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Multiple simultaneous occupants.} With one person the channel
        perturbation can be attributed to one source. With two, the
        superposition is not separable by a classifier trained on
        single-occupancy data, and the labelling problem itself becomes
        ill-posed.
  \item \emph{Through-wall operation.} Feasible on ESP32-class hardware
        \cite{strohmayer2023wall}, but it multiplies the collection matrix
        by another environmental factor that the available time does not
        cover.
  \item \emph{Pose or skeleton estimation.} A regression problem requiring
        synchronised ground truth (motion capture or camera) that is not
        available.
  \item \emph{Phase-based features.} Excluded for the hardware reason given
        in the problem statement.
  \item \emph{Transition classes.} Sit-down, stand-up, and lie-down are
        genuine activities but are excluded from the label set in this
        version; windows straddling a labelled boundary are trimmed rather
        than assigned to either neighbour.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Known limitations of the result.} Recognition accuracy is
environment-sensitive: a model trained in one room learns that room's
multipath as much as the activity, so a cross-environment result is
reported separately and is expected to be the weakest number in the report.
The dataset is collected by a four-person team with recruited volunteers, so
its subject diversity is bounded by what a student project can recruit; the
cross-subject figure should be read as a lower bound established on a small
subject pool, not as a population-level claim.
